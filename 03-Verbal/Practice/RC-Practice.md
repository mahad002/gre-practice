# GRE Verbal Practice: Reading Comprehension (10 original passages, 32 questions)

- What this is: 10 original passages with 32 questions in three sets. Set 1: two long passages (8 questions). Set 2: five medium passages (19 questions). Set 3: three short critical-reasoning paragraphs (5 questions).
- Formats, as on the real test: single-answer multiple choice (A to E, choose one), "select all that apply" (A to C, one to three correct, no partial credit), and select-in-passage (write down the first few words of the sentence you choose).
- Timing: about 1.5 to 2 minutes per question plus reading time. Target per set: Set 1 about 18 minutes, Set 2 about 30 minutes, Set 3 about 9 minutes.
- Method: read for structure (what is claimed, what is objected, where the author stands), and prove every answer from the text. Do not use outside knowledge.
- Write your answers on paper, then check the answers section at the bottom of this file. Do not scroll down while solving.

## Set 1: Long passages

### Passage 1 (questions 1 to 4)

Beneath most forests, the roots of trees are colonized by mycorrhizal fungi, organisms that supply their hosts with water and mineral nutrients in exchange for sugars produced by photosynthesis. Because a single fungus can colonize the roots of several trees at once, its filaments may physically link neighboring plants into what are called common mycorrhizal networks. That such networks exist is not in dispute. What has become contentious is the claim, popularized over the past three decades, that trees use these networks to share resources on a scale that matters ecologically: that large, old trees nourish shaded seedlings through fungal conduits, and even that they preferentially support their own offspring.

The claim rests on a small number of influential experiments. In the best known, researchers exposed one tree to carbon dioxide labeled with a rare isotope of carbon and later detected the isotope in the tissues of a neighboring tree. The result was widely taken to show that carbon had traveled from one plant to the other through the fungal network. Yet the experiment admits of other readings. Labeled carbon can move through the soil itself, released by roots and absorbed by neighbors without any fungal intermediary, and the quantities detected were in most cases small relative to what a seedling fixes through its own photosynthesis. Moreover, carbon found in the fungal tissue sheathing a seedling's roots is not necessarily carbon that the seedling itself can use.

Ecologists who have reviewed the field studies argue that the evidence for network-mediated benefits to seedlings is far thinner than its reputation suggests. In experiments that used fine mesh to prevent fungal filaments from connecting seedlings to mature trees, the excluded seedlings fared worse in some cases, better in others, and no differently in most. The reviewers also found that favorable results were cited far more often than unfavorable ones, and that later papers frequently described early findings in stronger terms than the original authors had used.

None of this shows that the hypothesis is false. Carbon transfer through fungal networks may yet prove significant in particular forests or under particular conditions, and the technical obstacles to measuring it in the field are formidable. But the episode is instructive. An idea that is vivid, that flatters a wish to see cooperation in nature, and that is easy to convey to the public can acquire, through repetition, a solidity that the data never conferred on it. The appropriate response is neither to cling to the idea nor to discard it, but to design the experiments that could actually settle the matter.

### Passage 2 (questions 5 to 8)

Economic historians of early modern Europe long faced a puzzle. Probate inventories, the lists of possessions drawn up when a person died, show that from the mid-seventeenth century onward ordinary households in England and the Netherlands owned a growing array of goods: clocks, mirrors, printed cottons, pottery for the newly fashionable drinks of tea and coffee. Yet estimates of daily wages for the same period show little sustained improvement. How could families whose pay was stagnant fill their homes with more things?

The historian Jan de Vries proposed an answer that he called the "industrious revolution." Households, he argued, did not earn more per day; they worked more days, and more of their members worked for money. Time once given to leisure, or to producing food and clothing for the family's own use, was redirected toward labor that earned cash, and the cash was spent on goods the market offered. On this account the change began with desire: new consumer goods tempted households to reorganize their time. The industrious revolution thus preceded the Industrial Revolution and helped prepare the ground for it, by creating both a disciplined labor force and a mass of customers before the factories arrived.

The thesis has proved fertile, but it has not gone unchallenged. Its most direct support comes from studies that reconstruct working time from indirect sources, such as the testimony of court witnesses who mentioned what they had been doing at a given hour; these suggest that the English working year lengthened considerably during the eighteenth century, in part because traditional holidays fell out of observance. Critics do not, for the most part, dispute that people worked more. They question why. If the price of bread rose faster than wages, as it did in several decades of the period, longer hours may reflect not the lure of new comforts but the pressure of old necessities. A family that sends its children to a workshop in order to eat is industrious in a sense quite different from the one de Vries intended.

The disagreement is hard to resolve because the two motives leave similar traces in the records. A longer working year looks the same in a court deposition whether it was chosen or compelled. The inventories, moreover, describe chiefly those who had property worth listing, and so may tell us least about the poorest households, for whom compulsion is most plausible. It may be that both accounts are correct for different strata of society, in which case the phrase "industrious revolution" names not one phenomenon but two.

When Emily Dickinson's poems first appeared in print in 1890, four years after her death, her editors smoothed them considerably: unconventional dashes became commas and periods, approximate rhymes were made exact, and titles were supplied for poems that had none. Not until 1955 did a scholarly edition restore the texts as they stand in her manuscripts. It has since become customary to treat the early editors as vandals who mistook originality for error. That verdict is too simple. The 1890 volume went through numerous printings within a few years, and it is doubtful that verse so strange to contemporary ears would have found those readers unaltered; the editors arguably secured the audience that made later restoration worth undertaking. To replace them is to resolve what the poet left deliberately unresolved. The editors were, in short, poor interpreters but effective advocates, and the reputation they are now accused of distorting is one they helped to create.

### Passage 4 (questions 13 to 16)

In the textbook model of a competitive labor market, a minimum wage set above the prevailing wage must reduce employment: firms, able to hire all the workers they want at the market rate, will hire fewer when that rate is forced upward. The prediction changes, however, if employers possess what economists call monopsony power, the ability to set wages rather than merely accept them, as may happen where a few firms dominate local hiring or where workers find it costly to change jobs. Such an employer holds wages down by hiring fewer workers than it otherwise would, since attracting an additional worker means raising pay for everyone already employed. A legal minimum removes that incentive. Up to a point, the firm required to pay more will also choose to hire more. The qualification matters: a minimum set high enough will reduce employment under either model. The dispute between the models, then, is not over whether minimum wages can destroy jobs but over how high they can rise before they begin to do so, and that is a question that theory alone cannot answer.

In 1973 the sociologist Mark Granovetter advanced a claim that seemed to defy common sense: in finding a job, a person's casual acquaintances are often more useful than close friends. His reasoning was structural rather than psychological. Close friends tend to know one another and to move in the same circles, so the information they hold is largely information one already has. Granovetter never claimed that acquaintances are more willing to help; friends are surely more motivated. His point was that motivation is useless without novel information to convey. For decades the thesis rested mainly on surveys and was difficult to test directly. A recent large-scale experiment on a professional networking site, which varied the contacts suggested to millions of users, lent it support with a refinement: the ties most likely to lead to a new job were neither the strongest nor the very weakest, but those of moderate weakness.

The thesis survives chiefly as a cautionary example of technological determinism: the assumption that a tool, once available, dictates the uses to which a society will put it.

## Answers and explanations

### Set 1 answers (long passages)

Quick key: 1 B, 2 A B C, 3 "The reviewers also found ...", 4 C, 5 B, 6 A C, 7 B, 8 B

**1. B.** Proof: the passage accepts that networks exist ("not in dispute"), then examines the sharing claim and finds the evidence "far thinner than its reputation suggests", ending with a lesson about ideas that gain "a solidity that the data never conferred". Tempting wrong answer: C is too strong. The last paragraph says "None of this shows that the hypothesis is false." E is wrong because the passage blames repetition and citation habits among researchers as well, and journalists are never named.

**2. A, B and C (all three).** Proof for A: "Labeled carbon can move through the soil itself ... without any fungal intermediary." Proof for B: "the quantities detected were in most cases small relative to what a seedling fixes through its own photosynthesis." Proof for C: "carbon found in the fungal tissue sheathing a seedling's roots is not necessarily carbon that the seedling itself can use." Trap: many students assume a select-all question never has all three correct. It can.

**3. "The reviewers also found that favorable results were cited far more often than unfavorable ones ..."** Proof: this sentence is about citation and description by later papers. Tempting wrong answer: the mesh sentence ("In experiments that used fine mesh ...") describes a pattern in the results themselves, which the question excludes.

**4. C.** Proof: doubt is shown by "far thinner than its reputation suggests"; openness is shown by "None of this shows that the hypothesis is false" and "may yet prove significant", and by the call for decisive experiments. Tempting wrong answer: A ignores "neither to cling to the idea nor to discard it". E is too optimistic: "may yet prove" is a possibility, not a prediction.

**5. B.** Proof: the paragraph sets growing possessions against flat wages and ends with a question ("How could families whose pay was stagnant fill their homes with more things?"). The second paragraph begins "proposed an answer". Tempting wrong answer: A, because the first paragraph states a puzzle, not a thesis, and the rest of the passage qualifies de Vries without refuting him.

**6. A and C.** Proof for A: "Critics do not, for the most part, dispute that people worked more." Proof for C: the critics' argument rests on bread prices that rose faster than wages, "as it did in several decades of the period". B is wrong: it is de Vries's own explanation, and it is exactly what the critics question ("They question why").

**7. B (productive).** Proof: "The thesis has proved fertile, but it has not gone unchallenged", followed by a description of the studies and debate it generated. A fertile thesis is one that produces further work. Tempting wrong answer: A (inventive) describes a creative mind, not what a thesis gives rise to. D (persuasive) is contradicted by "not gone unchallenged". Vocab: *lush* = growing thickly, the literal sense of fertile land.

**8. B.** Proof: "The inventories ... describe chiefly those who had property worth listing, and so may tell us least about the poorest households, for whom compulsion is most plausible." Tempting wrong answer: A is a reasonable real-world criticism of inventories, but the passage never makes it. Stay inside the text.
