# Reading Comprehension (RC): lesson

Reading Comprehension is a substantial part of Verbal, but ETS does not promise a fixed number of questions or passages. Passages vary in length and include both passage-based reading and short critical-reasoning arguments. This is where a 160+ is often won or lost, and it rewards method more than memorized vocabulary.

Study time: one evening for this lesson, then `Practice/RC-Practice.md` over about 6 sessions.

## 1. The one rule

**The correct answer is always proven by words in the passage.** You are not asked what is true in the real world or what is likely. If you cannot point to the line that supports an option, it's wrong. Four options are wrong for a specific reason, and your job is to find that reason.

## 2. How to read a passage

1. **Read for structure, not for detail.** As you read, ask: what is the author's point, and what is each part doing (old view, new evidence, objection, conclusion)?
2. **Note the turns.** Words like *however, but, yet, recently, in fact* show where the author's own view begins. The main point is very often right after a "however".
3. **Track opinions.** Who believes what? Keep "critics say", "traditional scholars held" and "the author argues" separate.
4. **For a long passage, write a 3–5 word note per paragraph** on your scratch paper ("P1: old theory", "P2: new data vs it", "P3: author: both partly right").
5. **Don't memorise details.** You'll go back to the passage for them anyway.
6. Before the questions, say the **main point in one sentence** in your head.

Reading time: about 1 minute for a short passage, 2.5–3 minutes for the long one.

## 3. Question types

| Type | How to recognise it | How to answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Main idea / primary purpose** | "The passage is primarily concerned with…" | Match your one-sentence summary. Reject options that cover only one paragraph or go beyond the passage. Check the first verb ("criticise", "describe", "reconcile"). |
| **Detail** | "According to the passage…" | Go back and find the line. The answer is a paraphrase of it. |
| **Inference** | "It can be inferred…", "The author would most likely agree…" | Choose what **must** be true from the text. It is usually a small, safe step, not a bold claim. |
| **Function** | "The author mentions X in order to…" | Ask why the sentence is there. Read the sentence before it. Is it an example, an objection, a concession? |
| **Vocabulary in context** | "In the context, 'check' most nearly means…" | Put your own word in the sentence first. It's usually not the most common meaning. |
| **Author's attitude** | "The author's attitude towards X is…" | Look at the adjectives and adverbs the author uses. GRE authors are rarely extreme: "qualified approval" beats "total enthusiasm". |
| **Select all that apply** | Three options, square boxes | Treat each option as a separate true/false question. One, two or all three may be correct. |
| **Select the sentence** | "Select the sentence that…" | Use your paragraph notes to find the area, then test each sentence against the description. |
| **Critical reasoning** | Short paragraph + "strengthens", "weakens", "assumption", "explains the discrepancy" | See section 5. |

## 4. The five wrong-answer types

1. **Too extreme:** uses *always, never, all, only, proves, completely* when the passage says *often, some, suggests*.
2. **Out of scope:** sounds sensible, but the passage never says it.
3. **Opposite:** reverses what the passage says, or gives the view the author rejects.
4. **True but wrong question:** it's in the passage, but it doesn't answer this question.
5. **Mixed up:** takes real words from the passage and combines them into a false statement. Options that copy the passage's exact words are often traps. The right answer is usually a paraphrase.

When two options are left, find the **one word** that makes one of them wrong.

## 5. Critical reasoning (argument questions)

Every argument has **evidence → conclusion**, and a **gap** between them (the assumption).

1. Find the **conclusion** (look for *thus, therefore, so, clearly, should*).
2. Find the **evidence**.
3. Ask: **what must be true for this evidence to lead to this conclusion?** That's the assumption.

- **Weaken:** attack the assumption. Classic ways: another cause explains the result, the sample is not representative, the cause and effect are reversed, the comparison is unfair.
- **Strengthen:** support the assumption, or rule out another explanation.
- **Assumption:** use the negation test. If the option were false, would the argument fall apart? Then it's the assumption.
- **Explain the discrepancy:** find the option that makes both surprising facts true at the same time.
- **Bold sentence role:** label each bold part as conclusion, evidence, opposing view or concession, then match the labels.

**Example.** *A city put speed cameras on ten roads. The next year, accidents on those roads fell by 20%. So the cameras made the roads safer.*
Conclusion: cameras caused the fall. Gap: nothing else caused it. Weaken: "Accidents fell by 20% across the whole city that year, including on roads with no cameras." Strengthen: "On similar roads with no cameras, accidents didn't fall."

## 6. Timing for a Verbal section

- Section 1: 12 questions in 18 minutes. Section 2: 15 questions in 23 minutes. That's about **1.5 minutes per question**, including reading.
- A good order: **do the Sentence Equivalence and Text Completion questions first** (fast points), then the short passages, then the long passage. You can move freely within a section and mark questions for review.
- A long passage with 3–4 questions deserves 6–7 minutes in total. Don't skip it. Its questions are worth the same as the others.
- With 1 minute left, make sure every question has an answer. There's no penalty for a wrong one.

## 7. Building reading stamina (10 minutes a day from week 4)

GRE passages are dense academic writing. Read one article a day from sources like **Aeon, The Economist, Scientific American, Arts & Letters Daily, or The New Yorker**, in a field you don't enjoy (art history, biology, philosophy). After each article, say its main point and the author's attitude in one sentence each.
