# Analytical Writing: the Issue essay

One essay, **30 minutes**, scored **0–6** in half points. It's the first thing on the test. Target: **4.5 or higher** (4.5 is roughly the top 20% of test-takers). The old "Argument" essay was removed in 2023, so this is the only writing task.

The plan gives writing limited but deliberate time: this guide, two illustrative samples in `02-Sample-Essays.md`, the scorecard in `03-Essay-Scorecard.md`, and **four timed practice essays**.

## 1. What the graders want

Each essay is scored by a trained human and by ETS's e-rater software. A 5–6 essay:

1. **Takes a clear position** on the issue and keeps it all the way through.
2. **Follows the specific instruction** under the prompt (there are six versions; see section 3).
3. **Develops the position with specific examples** and explains how each example proves the point.
4. **Deals with the other side** (a counter-argument or a case where the claim fails).
5. **Is organised**: clear paragraphs, topic sentences, linking words.
6. **Is written in clean, varied English.** A few small errors are fine.

Length and paragraph count are not scored directly. Many strong practice responses land around 450–600 words and four to five paragraphs, but development and control matter more than hitting either number.

## 2. The 30-minute plan

| Minutes | What |
|---|---|
| 0–2 | Read the prompt and the instruction. Underline what the instruction asks for. |
| 2–5 | Pick your side (the one with better examples, not the one you believe). Note the thesis, 2 examples for it, and 1 counter-argument. |
| 5–26 | Write. About 4 minutes for the intro, 5 minutes per body paragraph, 2 for the conclusion. |
| 26–30 | Proofread. Fix spelling, missing words and repeated words. The test's editor has **no spell-check**. |

## 3. The six instruction types

All of them need the same essay with a small change of focus.

| The instruction says… | What to add |
|---|---|
| "…discuss the extent to which you agree or disagree… consider ways in which the statement might or might not hold true" | Show a case where the statement holds and one where it doesn't. |
| "…describe specific circumstances in which adopting the recommendation would or would not be advantageous" | Give concrete situations for each. |
| "…address the most compelling reasons and/or examples that could be used to challenge your position" | Make the counter-argument paragraph strong, then answer it. |
| "…address both of the views presented" | Discuss view 1 and view 2, then say which one you support more. |
| "…discuss the extent to which you agree with the claim and the reason on which that claim is based" | Judge the claim **and** the reason separately. |
| "…consider the possible consequences of implementing the policy" | Write about the results the policy would have, good and bad. |

## 4. The template

This is a dependable practice structure, not a required ETS format.

**Paragraph 1: introduction (3–4 sentences)**
- One sentence that restates the issue in your own words.
- One sentence that admits the other side has a point.
- **Thesis:** your position with a condition. ("While X is true in some cases, I largely agree/disagree that … because A and B.")

**Paragraph 2: strongest reason**
- Topic sentence that states the reason.
- A specific example (a named person, company, event or study).
- 2–3 sentences that explain **how** the example proves the reason.

**Paragraph 3: second reason**
- The same structure, with an example from a different field.

**Paragraph 4: the other side**
- "Admittedly, …" or "Critics would argue that …" Give the best opposing case fairly.
- "However, …" Show why it doesn't defeat your position, or say exactly when it applies.

**Paragraph 5: conclusion (2–3 sentences)**
- Restate the thesis in new words, with the condition. End with one sentence on why the issue matters.

Useful linking phrases: *Consider, for instance, … · This case shows that … · By contrast, … · Admittedly, … · Nevertheless, … · In short, …*

## 5. Examples to prepare in advance

Prepare 8–10 examples you know well. Each one works for many prompts.

| Example | Works for prompts about |
|---|---|
| Building and leading a team of 9 from scratch (your own experience) | leadership, teamwork, learning by doing |
| AI tools changing how software is written | technology, education, skills |
| Kodak inventing the digital camera and ignoring it | change, tradition, risk |
| Netflix moving from DVDs to streaming | innovation, long-term thinking |
| Galileo and the Church | authority vs evidence, dissent |
| Semmelweis and hand-washing in hospitals | experts being wrong, resistance to new ideas |
| Penicillin (Fleming's accident) | chance in discovery, curiosity-driven research |
| The Apollo programme | government funding, ambitious goals |
| Edhi Foundation in Pakistan | individuals vs institutions, public service |
| Standardised tests like the GRE itself | measuring ability, fairness in education |
| Social media and attention | technology's costs, regulation |
| Singapore under Lee Kuan Yew | leadership, planning vs freedom |

## 6. Language tips for a 5+

- Use a mix of short and long sentences.
- Use precise verbs (*undermines, illustrates, overlooks*) in place of *is* and *has*.
- Don't use memorised fancy openings or quotes. Graders see through them.
- Don't write "I think" in every sentence. State the point directly.
- Don't make up statistics. A real, specific example is stronger.

## 7. Practice and feedback

1. Pick a prompt from the official **ETS Issue topic pool** (on ets.org, search "GRE Issue topic pool"). Your real prompt will come from that pool, so every practice essay is direct preparation.
2. Type it in a plain text editor with spell-check turned off, with a 30-minute timer.
3. Save it in `My-Essays/` as `Essay-1.md`, `Essay-2.md`, and so on, with the prompt on the first line.
4. Complete `03-Essay-Scorecard.md`, then ask Codex for a **provisional** review against the official rubric: “Grade my GRE essay in `05-Writing/My-Essays/Essay-1.md` against the ETS Issue rubric. Explain the provisional score and give the three highest-impact fixes.”
5. In the last week, read 15–20 prompts from the pool and plan each one for 3 minutes (thesis + examples) without writing it.
