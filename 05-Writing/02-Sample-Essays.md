# Two illustrative strong essays

These are original teaching examples, not ETS responses and not independently scored. Both prompts are written in the style of the ETS pool. Read each essay once for the argument, then again with the notes at the end to see how it is built.

---

## Sample 1

**Prompt:** *To become effective, a leader must be willing to abandon popular opinion and make decisions that most people disagree with.*
**Instruction:** Write a response in which you discuss the extent to which you agree or disagree with the statement and explain your reasoning for the position you take. In developing and supporting your position, you should consider ways in which the statement might or might not hold true.

**Essay (about 520 words)**

Leaders are judged by results, and results often depend on choices that looked wrong to most people when they were made. The statement is right that a leader who only follows the crowd is not leading at all. Yet it goes too far if it suggests that defying popular opinion is a mark of good leadership in itself. A leader must be willing to make unpopular decisions when the evidence demands them, but should treat wide disagreement as a warning to check the reasoning, not as proof of courage.

The strongest support for the statement comes from cases where the majority could not yet see what the leader saw. When Netflix decided to move away from its profitable DVD-by-mail business and towards streaming, customers complained, investors sold their shares, and commentators called the decision reckless. The company's leaders held their course because they understood that the cost of delivering video online was falling quickly, a trend that the average subscriber had no reason to follow. Within a decade the DVD business had almost vanished, and the unpopular decision had saved the company. A leader who had waited for agreement would have waited too long.

Unpopular decisions matter even more when the majority is not just uninformed but mistaken. In the 1840s the physician Ignaz Semmelweis showed that deaths in his maternity ward fell sharply when doctors washed their hands. Most of his colleagues rejected the idea, partly because it implied that they themselves had been causing deaths. Semmelweis enforced the practice in his own ward against the firm opinion of his profession, and he was right. In such cases, popularity measures comfort, not truth.

Admittedly, the statement can fail badly in practice. Many leaders have ignored public opposition and been wrong, and their confidence made the damage worse. A chief executive who pushes through a merger that employees, customers and analysts all oppose is far more often making an error than showing vision. Moreover, in a democracy, a leader who repeatedly overrides the public loses the trust that makes any decision workable. Disagreement carries information: people who object often know something about costs and risks that the leader has not considered. The lesson of Netflix and Semmelweis is therefore not that leaders should disregard opinion, but that they should weigh it against evidence, and explain their reasons when the two conflict.

In short, willingness to be unpopular is a necessary quality in a leader, but it is not a sufficient one. Effective leaders are distinguished less by how often they defy the majority than by how well they judge when defiance is justified. Because the most important decisions are usually the contested ones, that judgment, more than boldness alone, is what separates leadership from stubbornness.

## Sample 2

**Prompt:** *Governments should fund scientific research only when its practical benefits are clear in advance.*
**Instruction:** Write a response in which you discuss your views on the policy and explain your reasoning for the position you take. In developing and supporting your position, you should consider the possible consequences of implementing the policy and explain how these consequences shape your position.

**Essay (about 500 words)**

Public money is limited, and it seems reasonable to ask that research paid for by taxpayers should promise them something in return. A policy of funding only research with clear practical benefits would, however, have consequences opposite to those it intends. Because the most valuable discoveries are rarely predictable, such a policy would save money in the short term while slowly closing off the sources of future benefit. I therefore disagree with it, although I accept that practical value should guide part of any research budget.

The first consequence of the policy would be the loss of discoveries that nobody could have planned. Alexander Fleming was not looking for a medicine when he noticed that mould had killed the bacteria in one of his dishes, yet penicillin went on to save millions of lives. Likewise, the physicists who developed quantum mechanics in the 1920s were trying to understand the atom, not to build anything. Their work later made possible the transistor, and with it every computer and telephone in use today. Under the proposed policy, neither project would have qualified for support, since neither could show a benefit in advance.

A second consequence would be a change in the behaviour of scientists themselves. If funding depended on promised applications, researchers would learn to exaggerate the usefulness of their proposals and to avoid risky questions whose answers might lead nowhere. Research would drift towards small, safe improvements of existing products. Private companies already fund that kind of work, because they can profit from it. Government money is most valuable precisely where the market will not invest: in basic questions with uncertain and distant returns.

Supporters of the policy could reply that governments face urgent problems, and that money spent on studying distant galaxies cannot be spent on hospitals. This concern is serious, and it justifies directing a substantial share of public funds at defined goals, as was done with remarkable speed in the development of vaccines against COVID-19. Yet that very success depended on decades of earlier, unglamorous research into messenger RNA, which for years attracted little interest because it had no obvious use. Targeted research draws on a stock of basic knowledge, and the policy would stop that stock from being refilled.

The likely result of funding only predictable research would thus be a science that is cheaper, safer and steadily less productive. A wiser policy would divide support between applied projects with clear aims and basic research judged by its quality alone. Governments should demand practical benefits from science as a whole, but they cannot demand them, in advance, from each experiment.

## Why these model strong features

1. **Clear, qualified thesis** at the end of paragraph 1 ("necessary but not sufficient"; "disagree, although…").
2. **Each essay answers its own instruction**: sample 1 shows where the statement holds and fails, and sample 2 is organised around consequences.
3. **Specific examples** (Netflix, Semmelweis, Fleming, mRNA), each followed by a sentence that says what it proves.
4. **A fair counter-argument paragraph** that is then answered and used to sharpen the thesis.
5. **Topic sentences** start each body paragraph, and linking words (*Admittedly, Moreover, Yet, Likewise*) guide the reader.
6. **A conclusion that adds something** in place of simply repeating the introduction.

Copy the structure, not the sentences.
