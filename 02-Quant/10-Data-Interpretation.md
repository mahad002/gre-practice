# Data Interpretation

Data Interpretation questions share charts or tables in sets. ETS does not promise a fixed number per section. The mathematics is mainly percents, ratios, averages and comparisons; points are often lost by reading the wrong row, unit or chart, or by confusing percent with percentage points.

Important: on the real test the data is shown as graphics (bar charts, line graphs, pie charts, scatterplots, boxplots). Markdown cannot draw them, so this file gives the data as tables and, for several sets, also describes the chart in words. Use this file to learn the methods. Then rehearse real chart reading on the official ETS practice tests (the free POWERPREP Online tests and the ETS Official Guide), where the charts look exactly like test day.

Study plan: about 2 study days. Day 1: sections 1 to 3 and practice sets 1 to 3 (questions 1 to 12). Day 2: practice sets 4 to 6 (questions 13 to 24), review, and one official ETS Data Interpretation set under time pressure. Aim for about 1 minute 45 seconds per question.

## 1. What you must know

**How the sets work**

1. A set shows one or more charts or tables followed by multiple questions. The data stays on screen for every question in the set. Formats used are multiple choice with one answer, select all that apply, and numeric entry. There is no Quantitative Comparison inside a set.
2. Spend 20 to 30 seconds on the data before the first question. Read the title, the axis labels, the units ("in thousands", "in millions of dollars", "percent"), the legend, the year or group each chart covers, and any footnote. Do not study the numbers yet.
3. For each question, first find which chart, which row or bar and which year it asks about. Put your finger (or cursor) on it. Then calculate.
4. Data charts ARE drawn to scale. You can read values from them and estimate by eye.

**Chart types**

5. Table: exact values. The risk is reading the wrong row or column. Example: asked for 2023 but reading the 2022 column.
6. Bar chart: the height of a bar is the value. Read it against the axis gridlines. If a bar ends between gridlines at 100 and 150, slightly below the middle, read about 120.
7. Stacked bar: each bar is split into segments. The top of the bar is the total. A segment's value is its top minus its bottom. Example: a segment that runs from 90 to 135 is worth 45.
8. Line graph: shows change over time. A steeper segment means a bigger change in amount, not necessarily a bigger percent change.
9. Pie chart: shows shares of one total, usually in percent. Amount = percent * total. You need the total to get amounts. A sector's central angle = percent * 3.6 degrees. Example: 10 percent is 36 degrees.
10. Two pie charts with different totals: the percents cannot be compared as amounts. Example: 30 percent of 8,000 equals 24 percent of 10,000.
11. Scatterplot: each point is one item with an x-value and a y-value. A trend line (line of best fit) summarises the pattern and is used for predictions. Points above the trend line are higher than predicted. To count points with y > x, draw the line y = x and count the points above it.
12. Boxplot: shows least value, Q1, median, Q3, greatest value. Each of the four pieces holds about 25 percent of the data. It shows neither the mean nor the number of values. (See the Statistics file.)

**The calculations**

13. Percent of: part / whole * 100. Example: 200 out of 4,000 is 5 percent.
14. Percent change = (new - old) / old * 100. The old value is always the base. Example: from 80 to 110: 30 / 80 = 37.5 percent increase.
15. "A is what percent greater than B": (A - B) / B * 100. "A is what percent of B": A / B * 100. Example: 150 is 25 percent greater than 120, and 150 is 125 percent of 120.
16. Percent versus percentage point. If a share goes from 40 percent to 42 percent, it rose by 2 percentage points, but by 5 percent (2 / 40). Read which one is asked.
17. A share can fall while the amount rises, if the total grows. Example: 35 percent of 72 is 25.2 and 33 percent of 80 is 26.4.
18. Ratio: write the two numbers in the order asked and simplify. Example: 500 to 1,400 is 5 to 14.
19. Average of several values: add and divide by the count. To average percents from groups with different totals, go back to the amounts. Do not average the percents.
20. Percent of a percent: multiply. Example: restaurants are 25 percent of food spending and food is 20 percent of the budget, so restaurants are 5 percent of the budget.

**Estimation and calculator strategy**

21. "Approximately" or "closest to" means estimate. Look at the answer choices first. If they are far apart (10, 25, 40, 70, 160 percent), round hard. If they are close together (17.1, 17.3, 17.5), use the calculator.
22. Use benchmark fractions: 1/8 = 12.5 percent, 1/6 is about 16.7 percent, 1/3 is about 33.3 percent, 3/8 = 37.5 percent, 2/3 is about 66.7 percent.
23. To compare fractions without dividing, cross-multiply, or compare each to a benchmark. Example: is 270 more than 25 percent of 1,000? 25 percent is 250, so yes.
24. Use the on-screen calculator for awkward division and multi-digit multiplication. Do not use it for things like 10 percent of 4,000. Every calculator entry costs time and is a chance for a typing error.
25. Drop matching units before you calculate, then put them back. Example: 180 thousand units * $350 = 63,000 thousand dollars = $63 million.
26. For "which year had the greatest percent increase", do not compute every year. Rule out the years with a decrease or a clearly small rise, then compute the two or three candidates.

## 2. Worked examples

### Data set A (examples 1 to 3)

Revenue of a company by division, in millions of dollars.

| Division | 2022 | 2023 |
|---|---|---|
| Hardware | 120 | 150 |
| Software | 80 | 110 |
| Services | 60 | 54 |
| Licensing | 40 | 46 |
| Total | 300 | 360 |

**Example 1 (Multiple choice, one answer)**

By what percent did Software revenue increase from 2022 to 2023?

(A) 27.3%  (B) 30%  (C) 37.5%  (D) 40%  (E) 137.5%

Solution:
1. Change = 110 - 80 = 30.
2. Base = the old value, 80.
3. 30 / 80 = 3/8 = 37.5 percent. Answer: C.

The trap: dividing by the new value (30 / 110 = 27.3 percent), or reporting the change in dollars (30), or reporting 110 / 80 = 137.5 percent, which is "percent of", not "percent increase".

**Example 2 (Multiple choice, one answer)**

From 2022 to 2023, Hardware revenue as a percent of total revenue increased by approximately how many percentage points?

(A) 1.7  (B) 4.2  (C) 10  (D) 25  (E) 30

Solution:
1. 2022 share: 120 / 300 = 40 percent.
2. 2023 share: 150 / 360 = 41.7 percent.
3. Difference: 41.7 - 40 = 1.7 percentage points. Answer: A.

The trap: 25 is the percent increase in Hardware revenue itself. 4.2 is the percent change of the share (1.7 / 40). The question asks for the simple difference of the two shares.

**Example 3 (Select all that apply)**

Which divisions had a percent increase in revenue from 2022 to 2023 that was greater than the percent increase in the company's total revenue? Select all that apply.

(A) Hardware  (B) Software  (C) Services  (D) Licensing

Solution:
1. Total: 300 to 360 is a 20 percent increase. This is the bar to beat.
2. Hardware: 30 / 120 = 25 percent. Yes.
3. Software: 37.5 percent (from Example 1). Yes.
4. Services went down. No.
5. Licensing: 6 / 40 = 15 percent. No.
6. Answer: A, B.

Fastest method: multiply each 2022 value by 1.2 and compare with 2023. Hardware 144 < 150 yes. Software 96 < 110 yes. Licensing 48 > 46 no.

### Data set B (examples 4 and 5)

A scatterplot shows 8 stores of a retail chain. The horizontal axis is monthly advertising spending in thousands of dollars (0 to 10). The vertical axis is monthly sales in thousands of dollars (0 to 100). Each store is one point. A trend line is drawn through the points, and its equation is: sales = 8 * (advertising) + 16, with both in thousands of dollars.

The points on the scatterplot:

| Store | Advertising ($ thousands) | Sales ($ thousands) |
|---|---|---|
| A | 2 | 30 |
| B | 3 | 42 |
| C | 4 | 40 |
| D | 5 | 55 |
| E | 6 | 58 |
| F | 7 | 75 |
| G | 8 | 72 |
| H | 9 | 90 |

**Example 4 (Multiple choice, one answer)**

For how many of the 8 stores were monthly sales more than 10 times monthly advertising spending?

(A) 2  (B) 3  (C) 4  (D) 5  (E) 6

Solution:
1. For each store compare sales with 10 * advertising.
2. A: 30 > 20 yes. B: 42 > 30 yes. C: 40 = 40 no. D: 55 > 50 yes. E: 58 < 60 no. F: 75 > 70 yes. G: 72 < 80 no. H: 90 = 90 no.
3. Four stores. Answer: C.

On the real chart: imagine the line through (0, 0) and (10, 100). Count the points strictly above it.

The trap: counting stores C and H, which are exactly 10 times, not more than 10 times.

**Example 5 (Numeric entry)**

According to the trend line, what monthly sales, in dollars, are predicted for a store that spends $12,000 per month on advertising?

Solution:
1. Advertising of $12,000 is 12 in the units of the chart.
2. Sales = 8 * 12 + 16 = 112 (thousands of dollars).
3. The question asks for dollars: 112,000. Answer: 112,000.

The trap: entering 112. Always check the unit the question asks for against the unit of the axis.

### Data set C (examples 6 to 8)

Two boxplots are drawn on the same horizontal scale (40 to 100). They summarise the scores on one exam for Class X (40 students) and Class Y (60 students).

| | Least score | First quartile (Q1) | Median | Third quartile (Q3) | Greatest score |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Class X (40 students) | 42 | 58 | 70 | 80 | 96 |
| Class Y (60 students) | 50 | 62 | 68 | 74 | 90 |

```
            40      50      60      70      80      90      100
Class X       |-------------[=========|=======]------------|
             42            58        70      80           96
Class Y               |---------[====|====]-----------|
                     50        62   68   74          90
```

**Example 6 (Multiple choice, one answer)**

The interquartile range of the scores in Class X is how much greater than the interquartile range of the scores in Class Y?

(A) 2  (B) 10  (C) 12  (D) 14  (E) 22

Solution:
1. IQR = Q3 - Q1 = the length of the box.
2. Class X: 80 - 58 = 22. Class Y: 74 - 62 = 12.
3. Difference = 10. Answer: B.

The trap: 14 is the difference of the ranges (54 and 40). 22 and 12 are the two IQRs themselves.

**Example 7 (Select all that apply)**

Which of the following statements must be true? Select all that apply.

(A) At least 30 students in Class Y scored 68 or higher.
(B) Exactly half of the students in Class X scored below 70.
(C) The range of the scores in Class X is greater than the range of the scores in Class Y.
(D) More students scored above 80 in Class X than in Class Y.
(E) No student in Class Y scored below 50.

Solution:
1. A: the median of 60 scores is 68, so the upper 30 scores are all 68 or higher. True.
2. B: several students could have scored exactly 70, so the number below 70 could be fewer than 20. Not certain.
3. C: 96 - 42 = 54 and 90 - 50 = 40. True.
4. D: about a quarter of Class X (at most 10 students) scored above 80. In Class Y, the top quarter is 15 students with scores from 74 to 90, and the boxplot does not show how many of them are above 80. It could be 1 or it could be 14. Not certain.
5. E: the least score in Class Y is 50. True.
6. Answer: A, C, E.

The trap: reading more from a boxplot than it shows. It gives five numbers. It does not show where the values lie inside each piece, and it does not show the mean.

**Example 8 (Numeric entry)**

The mean score is 69 in Class X and 68.5 in Class Y. What is the mean score of all 100 students?

Solution:
1. Class X total: 40 * 69 = 2,760. Class Y total: 60 * 68.5 = 4,110.
2. Combined: 6,870 / 100 = 68.7. Answer: 68.7.

The trap: averaging 69 and 68.5 to get 68.75. The classes have different sizes.

## 3. Common traps

- Units. "In thousands" or "in millions" in a title or axis label changes the answer by a factor of 1,000. Check the unit the question wants.
- Percent versus percentage points. A share moving from 15 percent to 18 percent is up 3 points but up 20 percent.
- Wrong base in a percent change. Divide by the starting value, not the ending value.
- Comparing percents from charts with different totals as if they were amounts.
- A falling share does not mean a falling amount, and a constant share does not mean a constant amount.
- Different scales. Two charts side by side can have different axis ranges, and an axis may not start at 0. Read the numbers, not the visual height.
- Mixing up "percent of" and "percent greater than". 150 is 125 percent of 120 but only 25 percent greater.
- Averaging percents or averages across groups of different sizes.
- Answering for the wrong year or category, especially in the third question of a set when you are rushing.
- Over-calculating. If the choices are far apart, an estimate is enough. Save the calculator for close choices.
- Assuming things the chart does not show: the mean from a boxplot, individual values from a total, or a cause from a trend.
