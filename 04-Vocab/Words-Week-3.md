# Words: Week 3 (Days 11 to 15)

Common GRE words about effort, conflict, originality, intelligence and argument.

|---|---|---|---|
| assiduous | (adj) hard-working and persistent | Assiduous practice, three hours every day, made her the best pianist in the school. | diligent, industrious |
| diligent | (adj) steady and careful in work | A diligent student, he reviewed his notes after every class. | assiduous |
| industrious | (adj) hard-working | The industrious ants carried food to the nest from dawn to dusk. | diligent |
| indefatigable | (adj) never getting tired | An indefatigable campaigner, she knocked on doors for twelve hours a day. | tireless, tenacious |
| meticulous | (adj) very careful about small details | The meticulous editor checked every comma in the 600-page book. | fastidious, painstaking |
| fastidious | (adj) very fussy about detail or cleanliness | Fastidious about hygiene, he washed his hands after touching any door handle. | meticulous |
| painstaking | (adj) done with great care and effort | After years of painstaking research, she finally traced the author of the letter. | meticulous |
| conscientious | (adj) wishing to do one's work well and thoroughly | The conscientious nurse double-checked every dose before giving it. | diligent, scrupulous |
| zealous | (adj) full of eager enthusiasm for a cause | Zealous volunteers worked through the night to finish the shelter. | ardent, fervent |
| ardent | (adj) passionate; very enthusiastic | An ardent supporter of the team, he had not missed a match in twenty years. | fervent, zealous |
| fervent | (adj) showing strong, sincere feeling | She made a fervent appeal for donations, her voice shaking with emotion. | ardent |
| lethargic | (adj) lacking energy; sluggish | The heavy lunch left everyone lethargic and yawning through the afternoon meeting. | languid, listless |
| indolent | (adj) lazy; avoiding effort | The indolent clerk let papers pile up for weeks rather than file them. | lazy, slothful |
| apathetic | (adj) showing no interest or concern | Apathetic voters stayed home and said the result made no difference to them. | indifferent, listless |

## Day 12: Conflict and agreement

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| contentious | (adj) causing argument; fond of arguing | The contentious issue of school fees split the council for months. | controversial, polemical |
| belligerent | (adj) hostile and ready to fight | The belligerent customer shouted at staff and threatened to break the window. | bellicose, pugnacious |
| bellicose | (adj) eager to fight or go to war | The bellicose speech, full of threats, alarmed neighboring countries. | belligerent |
| pugnacious | (adj) quick to argue or fight | The pugnacious player was sent off for starting a third brawl. | belligerent, truculent |
| truculent | (adj) aggressively defiant | The truculent teenager met every request with a glare and a refusal. | pugnacious |
| polemical | (adj) strongly attacking an opinion in argument | His polemical essay attacked the policy line by line and spared no one. | contentious |
| antagonize | (v) to make someone hostile | Her constant corrections antagonized colleagues who had once liked her. | alienate, provoke |
| animosity | (n) strong hostility | The animosity between the brothers was so strong that they had not spoken in ten years. | enmity, rancor |
| enmity | (n) deep, lasting hatred between people | Decades of enmity between the clans ended with a marriage. | animosity |
| rancor | (n) long-lasting bitterness | She spoke of her former boss without rancor, having forgiven him long ago. | acrimony, animosity |
| acrimony | (n) bitterness in speech or manner | The divorce ended in acrimony, with each side insulting the other in court. | rancor |
| discord | (n) disagreement; lack of harmony | The inheritance sowed discord among the siblings, who stopped speaking to one another. | strife, dissonance |

| cordial | (adj) warm and friendly | Relations between the two firms stayed cordial despite the tough competition. | amicable, affable |

## Day 13: New, old and ordinary

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| novel | (adj) new and original | She proposed a novel method that no one in the field had tried before. | unprecedented, original |
| unprecedented | (adj) never done or known before | The flood was unprecedented, higher than any in the town's 300 years of records. | novel |
| nascent | (adj) just beginning to exist | The nascent industry had only three companies and no rules yet. | incipient, inchoate |
| incipient | (adj) beginning to develop | The doctor caught the incipient infection before it could spread. | nascent |
| burgeoning | (adj) growing or expanding quickly | The burgeoning city doubled its population in ten years. | flourishing, expanding |
| archaic | (adj) very old; no longer in use | The law was written in archaic language that no modern reader used. | antiquated, obsolete |
| obsolete | (adj) no longer used; out of date | Smartphones made paper maps nearly obsolete. | antiquated |
| antiquated | (adj) old-fashioned; outdated | The office still ran on antiquated computers from the 1990s. | archaic, obsolete |
| anachronism | (n) something placed in the wrong period of time | The wristwatch on the Roman soldier was an obvious anachronism in the film. | misplacement in time |
| banal | (adj) so ordinary as to be boring | The speech was full of banal remarks about how quickly time passes. | trite, hackneyed |
| trite | (adj) overused and unoriginal | Telling graduates to follow their dreams is trite advice that they have all heard before. | banal, hackneyed |
| hackneyed | (adj) used so often that it has lost its force | The hackneyed plot of a poor boy winning a rich girl bored the audience. | trite |
| prosaic | (adj) ordinary; lacking imagination | His prosaic description made even the volcano sound dull. | mundane, pedestrian |
| mundane | (adj) ordinary; everyday | She longed to escape mundane chores like washing dishes and paying bills. | prosaic |
| insipid | (adj) lacking flavor or interest | The soup was insipid, as if no salt or spice had come near it. | vapid, bland |
| vapid | (adj) offering nothing interesting or intelligent | The vapid conversation about the weather made him wish he had stayed home. | insipid, inane |
| derivative | (adj) copied from others; unoriginal | Critics called the album derivative, a pale copy of the band's heroes. | unoriginal, imitative |
| orthodox | (adj) following accepted beliefs or practices | The orthodox treatment is surgery, though some doctors now try newer methods. | conventional |
| idiosyncratic | (adj) peculiar to one individual | His idiosyncratic filing system made sense to no one but himself. | eccentric |
| anomaly | (n) something that differs from the normal | A snowstorm in July was an anomaly that puzzled weather experts. | aberration |

## Day 14: Wisdom and foolishness

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| erudite | (adj) having great knowledge from study | The erudite professor could quote texts in six languages from memory. | learned, scholarly |
| astute | (adj) quick to see how to gain an advantage | An astute investor, she sold her shares just before the market fell. | shrewd, perspicacious |
| shrewd | (adj) having sharp practical judgment | The shrewd trader spotted the fake at once and refused to pay. | astute |
| sagacious | (adj) wise and showing good judgment | The sagacious judge found a solution that satisfied both families. | judicious |
| perspicacious | (adj) having keen insight | A perspicacious reader will notice the clue hidden in the first chapter. | astute, discerning |
| judicious | (adj) showing good judgment; sensible | A judicious use of salt improves the dish, but too much ruins it. | prudent, sagacious |
| prudent | (adj) careful and sensible about the future | It is prudent to save part of your salary in case you lose your job. | judicious, circumspect |
| discerning | (adj) able to judge quality well | Discerning diners can taste the difference between fresh and frozen fish. | discriminating, perspicacious |
| acumen | (n) the ability to make quick, good judgments | Her business acumen turned a small stall into a national chain. | shrewdness |
| pragmatic | (adj) practical rather than idealistic | Taking a pragmatic view, he accepted a small gain now instead of waiting for a perfect deal. | practical |
| naive | (adj) lacking experience and too trusting | It was naive to believe that the stranger would return the money. | credulous, ingenuous |
| credulous | (adj) too ready to believe things | Credulous buyers believed the advert's claim that the pill would make them taller. | gullible |

| obtuse | (adj) slow to understand | He was too obtuse to notice that everyone was hinting he should leave. | dense |

| dilettante | (n) a person with only a shallow interest in a subject | A dilettante in painting, he bought the best brushes and gave up after a month. | dabbler, amateur |

## Day 15: Supporting, claiming and doubting

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| corroborate | (v) to confirm with evidence | A second witness corroborated her story by describing the same car and driver. | substantiate, validate |
| substantiate | (v) to provide evidence for | He could not substantiate his claim, having no documents or witnesses. | corroborate |
| validate | (v) to confirm as true or sound | The experiment validated the model by matching its predictions exactly. | corroborate |
| vindicate | (v) to clear of blame; to prove right | The DNA test vindicated the prisoner, who had insisted for years that he was innocent. | exonerate |
| underscore | (v) to emphasize | The accident underscored the need for better lighting on the road. | emphasize, highlight |
| advocate | (v, n) to support publicly; a supporter | Doctors advocate regular exercise as the best protection against heart disease. | champion, espouse |
| espouse | (v) to adopt or support a belief or cause | She espoused vegetarianism and persuaded half her office to try it. | advocate, champion |
| proponent | (n) a person who argues for something | A proponent of free trade, he argued against every new tariff. | advocate |
| contend | (v) to argue or claim; also to struggle | The lawyers contend that the contract was never valid. | aver, assert |
| posit | (v) to put forward as a basis for argument | The theory posits that the moon was once part of the earth. | postulate |
| conjecture | (n, v) a guess based on incomplete information | Without data, his estimate of the cost was mere conjecture. | surmise, speculation |
| surmise | (v) to guess from limited evidence | Seeing the wet umbrella, she surmised that it was raining outside. | conjecture |
| skeptical | (adj) doubting; not easily convinced | Skeptical investors asked to see the accounts before believing the profit figures. | dubious, incredulous |
| dubious | (adj) doubtful; of uncertain quality or truth | The email made the dubious claim that I had won a lottery I never entered. | skeptical, questionable |
| incredulous | (adj) unwilling or unable to believe | She gave him an incredulous stare when he said he had walked the whole way. | skeptical |
| tenuous | (adj) very weak or slight | The link between the two events was tenuous and rested on a single rumor. | flimsy |
| untenable | (adj) impossible to defend or maintain | After the leaked emails, his position became untenable and he resigned. | indefensible |
| plausible | (adj) seeming reasonable or likely | Her excuse was plausible, since the trains really had been delayed that morning. | credible |
| belie | (v) to give a false impression of; to contradict | His calm voice belied the panic he felt inside. | contradict, mask |
| debunk | (v) to expose as false | The documentary debunked the myth that we use only ten percent of our brains. | refute |
