# Words: Week 6 (Days 26 to 30)

The hardest group. Day 30 collects words that look like they mean something else, which the GRE uses as traps.

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|

| salubrious | (adj) good for health | He moved to the mountains for the salubrious air. | salutary |

| deleterious | (adj) harmful | Smoking has a deleterious effect on the lungs. | detrimental, pernicious |
| detrimental | (adj) causing damage | Too much screen time is detrimental to children's sleep. | deleterious |

| virulent | (adj) extremely harmful or infectious; bitterly hostile | The virulent strain spread to ten countries in a month. | deadly |
| precarious | (adj) not safe or secure; likely to fall or fail | The ladder stood in a precarious position on the edge of the roof. | unstable |
| arduous | (adj) needing great effort | The arduous climb took nine hours and left them exhausted. | onerous, laborious |
| onerous | (adj) heavy and hard to bear | The onerous duty of caring for three sick relatives left her no free time. | arduous, burdensome |
| laborious | (adj) needing much time and effort | Copying the book by hand was a laborious task that took a year. | arduous |
| cumbersome | (adj) heavy, awkward or slow to use | The cumbersome process required six forms and four signatures. | unwieldy |
| formidable | (adj) causing fear or respect by being powerful or difficult | She faced a formidable opponent who had not lost in five years. | daunting |

## Day 27: Courage, rashness, fear and caution

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| intrepid | (adj) fearless and adventurous | The intrepid explorer crossed the desert alone on foot. | dauntless |
| audacious | (adj) boldly daring; also disrespectfully bold | In an audacious move, the tiny firm offered to buy its largest rival. | brazen, bold |
| dauntless | (adj) showing no fear | Dauntless firefighters ran into the burning building again and again. | intrepid |
| brazen | (adj) bold and without shame | The brazen thief walked out of the shop carrying the television in daylight. | audacious |
| temerity | (n) reckless boldness | He had the temerity to ask for a raise on his second day at work. | audacity |
| impetuous | (adj) acting quickly without thinking | Her impetuous decision to quit, made in a moment of anger, was soon regretted. | rash |
| foolhardy | (adj) taking stupid risks | It was foolhardy to sail in such a storm without life jackets. | reckless, impetuous |
| fortitude | (n) courage in facing pain or hardship | She bore the long illness with fortitude and never lost her sense of humor. | mettle |
| mettle | (n) courage and strength of character | The final round tested the mettle of even the most experienced players. | fortitude |
| timorous | (adj) nervous and easily frightened | The timorous child hid behind her mother whenever a stranger spoke. | timid |
| craven | (adj) shamefully cowardly | The craven captain left the sinking ship before his passengers. | pusillanimous |
| pusillanimous | (adj) lacking courage; timid | Too pusillanimous to confront the bully, the council simply looked away. | craven |
| trepidation | (n) fear about something that may happen | She opened the exam results with trepidation, her hands shaking. | dread |
| apprehensive | (adj) worried about what might happen | He felt apprehensive before the surgery and could not sleep. | anxious |
| wary | (adj) cautious about possible danger | Wary of strangers, the dog kept its distance and watched. | chary, circumspect |

| chary | (adj) cautiously reluctant | Once cheated, he was chary of lending money to anyone again. | wary |

| qualm | (n) an uneasy feeling of doubt about an action | He had no qualms about lying if it helped him win. | misgiving |

## Day 29: Strong speech: attacking, praising, asserting and denying

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| harangue | (n, v) a long, forceful, critical speech | The coach delivered a twenty-minute harangue about laziness at half-time. | tirade, diatribe |
| diatribe | (n) a bitter verbal attack | The article was a diatribe against modern architecture, with not one kind word. | tirade |
| tirade | (n) a long, angry speech | She launched into a tirade against the airline when her bag was lost again. | harangue, diatribe |
| invective | (n) insulting or abusive language | The debate descended into invective, with each side calling the other liars. | abuse, vituperation |
| excoriate | (v) to criticize very severely | The judge excoriated the company for hiding the danger from its customers. | lambaste, castigate |
| calumny | (n) a false statement meant to damage a reputation | He sued the paper over the calumny that he had stolen from his own charity. | slander |
| opprobrium | (n) harsh public criticism or disgrace | The minister faced public opprobrium after the lies were exposed. | censure |
| approbation | (n) approval or praise | The plan won the approbation of the board, which voted for it unanimously. | acclaim |
| panegyric | (n) a speech or text of high praise | The biography reads like a panegyric, listing virtues and no faults. | eulogy |
| eulogy | (n) a speech praising someone, usually after death | At the funeral, her brother gave a moving eulogy about her kindness. | panegyric |
| platitude | (n) a dull remark used too often to be meaningful | The speech offered only platitudes about hard work and bright futures. | cliche |
| hyperbole | (n) deliberate exaggeration | To say he had told her a million times was hyperbole. | exaggeration |
| euphemism | (n) a mild word used in place of a harsh one | To say that someone has passed away is a euphemism for saying that the person has died. | polite substitute |
| caustic | (adj) bitterly sarcastic; able to burn | Her caustic review left the young actor in tears. | acerbic |
| acerbic | (adj) sharp and harsh in tone | His acerbic wit made people laugh and wince at once. | caustic |
| trenchant | (adj) sharp, clear and forceful | Her trenchant analysis cut through the confusion and named the real cause. | incisive |
| glib | (adj) fluent but shallow or insincere | The glib answer came too quickly to be the result of any real thought. | slick |

| recant | (v) to withdraw a former statement or belief | Under pressure, the scientist recanted his claim that the earth moves. | retract, repudiate |

## Day 30: Words that do not mean what they seem to mean

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| restive | (adj) restless and hard to control (not restful) | The crowd grew restive after waiting two hours for the delayed speaker. | restless, fidgety |
| noisome | (adj) foul-smelling; offensive (nothing to do with noise) | A noisome stench rose from the blocked drain. | noxious, fetid |
| disinterested | (adj) impartial; having no personal stake (not bored) | We need a disinterested judge who gains nothing whichever side wins. | impartial, unbiased |
| nonplussed | (adj) so surprised and confused that one cannot react | Nonplussed by the strange question, she stood silent, with no idea how to answer. | bemused, bewildered |
| inchoate | (adj) just begun and not fully formed | His inchoate plan was little more than a few notes on a napkin. | nascent, incipient |
| meretricious | (adj) attractive on the surface but worthless (not merit) | The meretricious film dazzled with effects but had nothing to say. | tawdry, specious |
| fulsome | (adj) excessive and insincere, especially of praise | His fulsome compliments were so exaggerated that nobody believed them. | effusive, excessive |
| tortuous | (adj) full of twists; complicated (torturous = very painful) | The tortuous mountain road had a bend every fifty meters. | convoluted, winding |
| venial | (adj) minor and easily forgiven (venal = corrupt) | Arriving five minutes late is a venial fault, hardly worth a mention. | pardonable |
| enormity | (n) extreme wickedness or seriousness (not just large size) | Only at the trial did the public grasp the enormity of his crimes. | atrocity, wickedness |
| fortuitous | (adj) happening by chance (not necessarily lucky) | Their meeting was fortuitous, as both happened to miss the same train. | accidental, chance |
| bemused | (adj) puzzled or confused (not amused) | Bemused by the contradictory signs, the tourists stood wondering which way to go. | nonplussed |
| peruse | (v) to read carefully and thoroughly (not to skim) | She perused the contract for an hour before signing. | scrutinize |
| officious | (adj) too eager to give orders or unwanted help (not official) | An officious clerk insisted on checking forms that needed no checking. | meddlesome |
| apposite | (adj) very suitable or relevant (not opposite) | Her apposite example made the abstract rule immediately clear. | apt |
| ingenious | (adj) clever and inventive (ingenuous = naive) | The ingenious device used a single spring to open, close and lock the door. | inventive |
| prodigious | (adj) remarkably great in size or degree (prodigal = wasteful) | The child had a prodigious memory and could recite whole books. | enormous |
| proscribe | (v) to forbid (prescribe = to order or recommend) | The law proscribes smoking in all public buildings. | prohibit, ban |
| ambivalent | (adj) having mixed feelings (ambiguous = unclear) | She felt ambivalent about the move, excited by the job but sad to leave friends. | torn, conflicted |
| discrete | (adj) separate and distinct (discreet = careful, tactful) | The course is divided into six discrete units, each with its own exam. | separate, distinct |
