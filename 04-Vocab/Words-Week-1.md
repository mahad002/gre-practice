# Words: Week 1 (Days 1 to 5)

The most common GRE words. Learn each day's group together, because the words in a group are near-synonyms or opposites.

## Day 1: Praise and criticism

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| laud | (v) to praise highly | Critics lauded the film as the best of the decade, and it won every major award. | extol, acclaim |
| extol | (v) to praise enthusiastically | The coach extolled her players' discipline in every interview and never missed a chance to praise them. | laud, acclaim |
| venerate | (v) to regard with deep respect | The villagers venerate the old teacher and still seek her advice on every important matter. | revere |
| revere | (v) to respect and admire deeply | Students revered the professor so much that they fell silent whenever he entered the room. | venerate |
| acclaim | (n, v) enthusiastic public praise; to praise publicly | The novel won wide acclaim, with reviewers calling it a masterpiece. | laud, approbation |
| adulation | (n) excessive admiration or praise | The singer grew tired of the adulation of fans who screamed at everything he did. | flattery, worship |
| censure | (v, n) to criticize formally and strongly (not censor) | The senate voted to censure the minister for misleading the public. | rebuke, reproach |
| castigate | (v) to criticize or punish severely | The editor castigated the reporter for publishing the story without checking a single fact. | lambaste, berate |
| berate | (v) to scold angrily and at length | The manager berated the waiter in front of customers for dropping one plate. | upbraid, castigate |
| rebuke | (v, n) to criticize sharply; a sharp criticism | The judge rebuked the lawyer for interrupting the witness yet again. | reproach, admonish |
| chastise | (v) to scold or punish | She chastised her son for lying and took away his phone for a week. | rebuke, castigate |
| admonish | (v) to warn or scold gently but firmly | The librarian admonished the children to keep their voices down. | rebuke, reproach |
| disparage | (v) to speak of as having little worth | He disparaged his rival's research, calling it shallow and unoriginal. | belittle, denigrate |
| denigrate | (v) to attack the reputation of unfairly | The campaign tried to denigrate the candidate by spreading rumors about her past. | disparage, vilify |
| deride | (v) to mock with contempt | Early critics derided the invention as a useless toy and laughed at its inventor. | scorn, ridicule |
| belittle | (v) to make seem small or unimportant | She belittled his promotion by saying that anyone could have got it. | disparage |
| vilify | (v) to describe as evil; to slander | The press vilified the banker as a greedy villain long before the trial began. | denigrate |
| lambaste | (v) to criticize harshly | Reviewers lambasted the sequel, calling it lazy, dull and far too long. | castigate, excoriate |
| reproach | (v, n) to express disappointment or blame | She reproached him for forgetting her birthday, though she sounded more hurt than angry. | rebuke, admonish |
| upbraid | (v) to scold severely | The captain upbraided the sailor for sleeping on watch. | berate, chastise |

## Day 2: Talkative vs. quiet

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| garrulous | (adj) talking too much, often about trivial things | Our garrulous neighbor kept us at the gate for an hour with stories about his cats. | loquacious, voluble |
| loquacious | (adj) very talkative | The normally loquacious host fell silent when asked about the scandal. | garrulous, voluble |
| verbose | (adj) using more words than needed | The verbose report took ten pages to say what one paragraph could have said. | prolix |
| voluble | (adj) talking fluently and rapidly | A voluble salesman, he could talk for minutes without pausing for breath. | loquacious, garrulous |
| prolix | (adj) tediously long and wordy | Readers gave up on the prolix novel, which spent fifty pages describing a single dinner. | verbose |
| effusive | (adj) expressing feelings in an unrestrained way | Her effusive thanks, repeated five times with hugs, embarrassed the shy doctor. | gushing, fulsome |
| bombastic | (adj) sounding grand but with little meaning | The bombastic speech was full of grand phrases and empty of actual plans. | pompous |
| circumlocution | (n) an indirect, roundabout way of speaking | Tired of the minister's circumlocution, the reporter demanded a simple yes or no. | wordiness, evasion |
| digress | (v) to wander from the main topic | The lecturer often digressed into stories about his travels and never finished the syllabus. | stray, deviate |
| eloquent | (adj) fluent and persuasive in speech or writing | Her eloquent plea moved the jury to tears and changed the verdict. | articulate |
| taciturn | (adj) habitually saying very little | The taciturn farmer answered every question with a nod or a single word. | reticent, laconic |
| reticent | (adj) unwilling to speak freely; reserved | He was reticent about his childhood and changed the subject whenever it came up. | taciturn |
| laconic | (adj) using very few words | Her laconic reply, a single "No," ended the two-hour debate. | terse, taciturn |
| terse | (adj) brief, sometimes to the point of rudeness | His terse email, just three words long, made her nervous. | curt, laconic |
| succinct | (adj) briefly and clearly expressed | The summary was succinct and covered the whole project in three clear sentences. | concise, pithy |
| concise | (adj) giving much information in few words | Keep your answer concise, because the examiner has only a minute to read it. | succinct |
| pithy | (adj) short and full of meaning | The essay ended with a pithy remark that readers quoted for years. | succinct |
| curt | (adj) rudely brief | Offended by her curt "Not now," he left without saying goodbye. | brusque, terse |
| brusque | (adj) abrupt and blunt in manner | The doctor's brusque manner upset patients, who felt rushed and unheard. | curt |
| brevity | (n) shortness of expression or of time | The speech was praised for its brevity, as it lasted barely two minutes. | concision |

## Day 3: Stubborn vs. flexible

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| obstinate | (adj) stubbornly refusing to change one's mind | The obstinate child refused to wear a coat even as snow began to fall. | obdurate, intransigent |
| obdurate | (adj) stubbornly unmoved by persuasion or pity | The landlord remained obdurate and ignored the family's pleas for one more week. | obstinate |
| intransigent | (adj) refusing to compromise | Talks collapsed because both sides were intransigent and would not give up a single demand. | obdurate, adamant |
| recalcitrant | (adj) stubbornly resisting authority or control | The recalcitrant student ignored every rule the school tried to enforce. | intractable, insubordinate |
| intractable | (adj) hard to control, manage or solve | The disease proved intractable and resisted every treatment the doctors tried. | recalcitrant |
| tenacious | (adj) holding firmly; not giving up easily | The tenacious reporter chased the story for three years until she had proof. | dogged, resolute |
| dogged | (adj) persistent in spite of difficulty | Through dogged effort, he passed the exam on his fifth attempt. | tenacious |
| adamant | (adj) refusing to be persuaded | She was adamant that she would not sign, whatever they offered her. | intransigent, resolute |
| steadfast | (adj) firmly loyal and unchanging | He remained a steadfast friend when everyone else had abandoned her. | resolute, abiding |
| resolute | (adj) firmly determined | Resolute in her decision to leave, she ignored every attempt to change her mind. | steadfast, adamant |
| dogmatic | (adj) stating opinions as certain truths, without evidence | The dogmatic professor dismissed any theory that differed from his own. | opinionated, doctrinaire |
| pliable | (adj) easily bent or influenced | The young recruits were pliable and accepted whatever the leader told them. | malleable |
| malleable | (adj) easily shaped or influenced | Children's minds are malleable, so early teaching leaves a lasting mark. | pliable |
| tractable | (adj) easy to control or deal with | The new horse was tractable and followed commands from the first day. | docile, amenable |
| amenable | (adj) willing to accept a suggestion | The manager was amenable to the change and approved it at once. | tractable, compliant |
| docile | (adj) quiet and easy to control | The once wild dog became docile after months of patient training. | tractable, meek |
| compliant | (adj) willing to obey rules or requests | Compliant employees followed the new policy without a single complaint. | amenable, docile |
| acquiesce | (v) to accept reluctantly without protest | Though she disliked the plan, she acquiesced rather than start another argument. | concede, capitulate |
| capitulate | (v) to stop resisting; to surrender | After a week of strikes, the company capitulated and raised wages. | acquiesce |
| concede | (v) to admit something is true; to give up | He finally conceded that his opponent had the stronger argument. | acquiesce |

## Day 4: Deceit and honesty

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| duplicity | (n) deliberate deceit; double-dealing | His duplicity was exposed when both partners found he had promised each of them the same contract. | guile |
| guile | (n) clever, sly deception | She used guile rather than force and tricked the guard into opening the door. | duplicity, cunning |
| disingenuous | (adj) not sincere; pretending to know less than one does | It was disingenuous of him to act surprised at news he had leaked himself. | insincere |
| mendacious | (adj) lying; untruthful | The mendacious witness changed his story three times under oath. | dishonest |
| spurious | (adj) false or fake, though seeming genuine | The claim rested on spurious data that no other laboratory could reproduce. | specious, bogus |
| specious | (adj) seeming right or true but actually false | The argument sounded convincing but was specious, since it ignored half the evidence. | fallacious, spurious |
| fallacious | (adj) based on a mistaken belief or bad logic | It is fallacious to assume that because two events occur together, one causes the other. | specious |
| feign | (v) to pretend to feel or have | He feigned illness to avoid the meeting and coughed loudly on the phone. | dissemble, fake |
| dissemble | (v) to hide one's real feelings or intentions | Unable to dissemble, she let her disappointment show plainly on her face. | feign |
| prevaricate | (v) to avoid telling the truth by being evasive | Asked directly whether he took the money, he prevaricated and talked about his busy week. | equivocate |
| equivocate | (v) to use vague language to avoid commitment | The candidate equivocated on taxes, giving answers that could mean anything. | prevaricate |
| charlatan | (n) a person falsely claiming skill or knowledge | The so-called healer was a charlatan who sold colored water as medicine. | fraud, quack |
| fabricate | (v) to invent in order to deceive | The researcher fabricated results for experiments he had never run. | concoct |
| candid | (adj) honest and direct | In a candid interview, she admitted the mistakes she had made as leader. | forthright, frank |
| forthright | (adj) direct and outspoken | He gave a forthright answer and said plainly that the plan would fail. | candid |
| ingenuous | (adj) innocent, trusting and sincere (not ingenious) | Her ingenuous belief that everyone tells the truth made her easy to fool. | artless, guileless |
| artless | (adj) natural and sincere; without deceit | The child's artless question, asked with no hidden motive, embarrassed the adults. | ingenuous, guileless |
| veracity | (n) truthfulness; accuracy | Reporters questioned the veracity of the statement after documents contradicted it. | truthfulness |
| guileless | (adj) honest and without cunning | Guileless and open, he never suspected that his partners were cheating him. | artless, ingenuous |
| scrupulous | (adj) very careful to do what is right; very thorough | A scrupulous accountant, she reported even the smallest error in her own favor. | conscientious, meticulous |

## Day 5: Change and stability

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| volatile | (adj) likely to change suddenly | Prices were so volatile that a stock could double and halve in the same week. | erratic, mercurial |
| capricious | (adj) changing mood or behavior without reason | The capricious director praised a scene one day and cut it the next. | fickle, whimsical |
| mercurial | (adj) quickly changing in mood | Her mercurial temper meant that laughter could turn to rage within minutes. | volatile, capricious |
| fickle | (adj) changing loyalties or interests often | Fickle fans who cheered the team last year now boo it after two losses. | capricious |
| erratic | (adj) irregular and unpredictable | The driver's erratic steering, swerving left and right, alarmed the police. | volatile |
| whimsical | (adj) playfully fanciful; decided by sudden impulse | On a whimsical impulse, he painted the front door bright purple. | capricious |
| vacillate | (v) to keep changing between opinions | She vacillated for weeks between the two job offers, unable to choose. | waver |
| waver | (v) to become unsteady or undecided | His confidence wavered when he saw how difficult the first question was. | vacillate |
| fluctuate | (v) to rise and fall irregularly | Temperatures fluctuate widely in the desert, from freezing nights to burning days. | vary |
| mutable | (adj) liable to change | Fashion is mutable, and what is admired today looks silly in a decade. | changeable |
| transient | (adj) lasting only a short time | The pain was transient and had disappeared by the next morning. | ephemeral, fleeting |
| ephemeral | (adj) lasting a very short time | Fame on the internet is ephemeral, and last month's star is already forgotten. | transient, fleeting |
| fleeting | (adj) passing very quickly | She caught only a fleeting glimpse of the bird before it vanished. | transient, ephemeral |
| transitory | (adj) not permanent | He reminded himself that the hardship was transitory and that better days would come. | transient |
| static | (adj) not moving or changing | Wages remained static for five years while prices kept rising. | stagnant |
| immutable | (adj) unable to be changed | The laws of physics are immutable, whatever politicians may wish. | unchangeable |
| stagnant | (adj) not flowing, growing or developing | The stagnant economy produced no new jobs for three years. | static |
| perennial | (adj) lasting or recurring for a long time | Traffic is a perennial problem in the city, complained about by every generation. | abiding, enduring |
| abiding | (adj) lasting a long time | Her abiding love of music stayed with her from childhood to old age. | steadfast, perennial |
| entrenched | (adj) firmly established and hard to change | The habit was so entrenched that no campaign could persuade people to give it up. | ingrained |
