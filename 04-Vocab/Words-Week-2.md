# Words: Week 2 (Days 6 to 10)

Still very common GRE words. Day 10 is special: ordinary words that the GRE uses in a less familiar second meaning.

## Day 6: Making things better vs. making things worse

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| ameliorate | (v) to make a bad situation better | New housing helped ameliorate the overcrowding in the city center. | alleviate, mitigate |
| alleviate | (v) to make pain or a problem less severe | The medicine alleviated her headache within minutes. | mitigate, assuage |
| mitigate | (v) to make less severe or harmful | Planting trees can mitigate the effects of flooding. | alleviate |
| assuage | (v) to ease an unpleasant feeling | Nothing could assuage his guilt over the accident. | allay, alleviate |
| mollify | (v) to calm someone's anger | The airline tried to mollify angry passengers with free meals. | placate, appease |
| placate | (v) to make less angry, often by giving in | He placated the crying child with a promise of ice cream. | mollify, appease |
| appease | (v) to calm by giving what is demanded | The king appeased the rebels by lowering their taxes. | placate |
| pacify | (v) to bring calm or peace to | Police were sent to pacify the crowd before the protest turned violent. | mollify |
| allay | (v) to reduce fear, doubt or worry | The pilot's calm announcement allayed the passengers' fears. | assuage |
| conciliate | (v) to win over; to end a disagreement peacefully | The manager tried to conciliate the two departments by meeting the concerns of each. | placate, appease |
| bolster | (v) to support or strengthen | The new evidence bolstered her case and convinced the doubtful jurors. | buttress |
| buttress | (v, n) to support or strengthen; a support | He buttressed his argument with statistics from three separate studies. | bolster |
| exacerbate | (v) to make a bad situation worse | Shouting at the frightened dog only exacerbated its aggression. | aggravate, compound |
| aggravate | (v) to make worse | Running on the injured ankle aggravated the damage. | exacerbate |
| compound | (v) to make worse by adding to (second meaning) | He compounded the error by lying about it afterwards. | exacerbate, aggravate |
| undermine | (v) to weaken gradually or secretly | Constant criticism undermined her confidence until she stopped speaking in meetings. | impair, erode |
| impair | (v) to damage or weaken | Lack of sleep impairs memory and slows reaction time. | undermine |
| debilitate | (v) to make very weak | The long illness debilitated him so much that he could barely stand. | enervate |
| enervate | (v) to drain of energy; to weaken (not energize) | The humid heat enervated the hikers, who could hardly lift their feet by noon. | debilitate |
| vitiate | (v) to spoil or reduce the value of | One false figure vitiated the whole study and made its conclusions worthless. | impair, undermine |

## Day 7: Clear vs. unclear

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| lucid | (adj) clear and easy to understand | Her lucid explanation made even quantum physics seem simple. | coherent, clear |
| explicit | (adj) stated clearly, leaving no doubt | The contract was explicit that payment was due on the first of each month. | unequivocal |
| unequivocal | (adj) leaving no doubt; not ambiguous | The answer was an unequivocal no, with no room for negotiation. | explicit |
| cogent | (adj) clear, logical and convincing | She made such a cogent case that even her opponents voted for the plan. | compelling |
| coherent | (adj) logical and consistent; easy to follow | After the accident he was too confused to give a coherent account of events. | lucid |
| elucidate | (v) to make clear; to explain | The diagram helped elucidate a process that words alone had left confusing. | explicate |
| explicate | (v) to explain in detail | The scholar spent a chapter explicating a single line of the poem. | elucidate |
| manifest | (adj, v) clearly visible; to show clearly | His relief was manifest in the broad smile that spread across his face. | patent, evince |
| ambiguous | (adj) having more than one possible meaning | The ambiguous sign left drivers unsure whether the road was closed or open. | equivocal |
| equivocal | (adj) deliberately unclear; open to two readings | His equivocal reply let both sides believe that he supported them. | ambiguous |
| obscure | (adj, v) little known; hard to understand; to hide | The poem's meaning remained obscure even to experts who studied it for years. | opaque, abstruse |
| opaque | (adj) not transparent; hard to understand | The legal language was so opaque that no ordinary reader could follow it. | obscure |
| abstruse | (adj) difficult to understand | The lecture was so abstruse that only two specialists in the audience followed it. | arcane, esoteric |
| arcane | (adj) known or understood by very few | He loved the arcane rules of medieval heraldry, which almost nobody else had studied. | esoteric |
| esoteric | (adj) meant for a small group with special knowledge | The journal publishes esoteric papers that only a handful of mathematicians can read. | arcane, abstruse |
| cryptic | (adj) mysterious; having a hidden meaning | She left a cryptic note of three words that nobody could explain. | enigmatic |
| enigmatic | (adj) puzzling; hard to interpret | His enigmatic smile gave no hint of whether he was pleased or angry. | cryptic |
| nebulous | (adj) vague; without clear form | The plan was still nebulous, with no dates, costs or names attached. | vague |
| convoluted | (adj) extremely complicated and hard to follow | The plot was so convoluted that viewers needed a chart to track who had betrayed whom. | tortuous |
| obfuscate | (v) to make unclear on purpose | The company used technical jargon to obfuscate the real cost of the loan. | obscure |

## Day 8: Pride and humility

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| haughty | (adj) proud and looking down on others | The haughty duchess refused to speak to the servants directly. | supercilious, imperious |
| supercilious | (adj) behaving as if superior to others | With a supercilious lift of his eyebrow, the waiter corrected my pronunciation. | haughty, condescending |
| condescending | (adj) treating others as less intelligent | His condescending tone, as if he were explaining to a child, annoyed the experienced engineers. | patronizing |
| patronizing | (adj) kind in a way that shows a sense of superiority | She gave him a patronizing pat on the head and told him it was a good try. | condescending |
| pompous | (adj) self-important and overly grand | The pompous official insisted on being addressed by his full title at all times. | pretentious, bombastic |
| pretentious | (adj) trying to seem more important or cultured than one is | The pretentious menu described plain toast as artisan grilled bread. | pompous |
| hubris | (n) excessive pride that leads to a fall | In his hubris, the general ignored every warning and marched his army into the trap. | arrogance |
| imperious | (adj) arrogantly giving orders | With an imperious wave of her hand, she dismissed the whole committee. | haughty |
| disdain | (n, v) the feeling that something is unworthy of respect | She looked at the cheap gift with disdain and pushed it aside. | scorn, contempt |
| scorn | (n, v) open contempt; to reject with contempt | The scientist scorned the idea and called it childish nonsense. | disdain, deride |
| contemptuous | (adj) showing a strong lack of respect | His contemptuous laugh made it clear what he thought of their proposal. | scornful, disdainful |
| aloof | (adj) distant and not friendly | She stayed aloof at parties and stood alone by the window. | detached, reserved |
| complacent | (adj) too satisfied with oneself to notice danger | Complacent after years of success, the firm ignored its new competitors until it was too late. | smug |
| diffident | (adj) shy because of a lack of confidence | Too diffident to raise his hand, he let others answer questions he knew well. | meek, self-effacing |
| self-effacing | (adj) not drawing attention to oneself; modest | The self-effacing winner gave all the credit to her team. | unassuming |
| unassuming | (adj) modest; not showy | Despite his fame, he lived in an unassuming house and drove an old car. | self-effacing |
| deferential | (adj) showing polite respect | The young lawyer was deferential to the judge and never once interrupted. | respectful |
| meek | (adj) quiet, gentle and easily imposed on | Too meek to complain, he ate the cold meal without a word. | docile, diffident |
| demure | (adj) quiet, modest and reserved | She gave a demure smile and lowered her eyes when praised. | modest |
| abase | (v) to lower in rank or dignity; to humble | He refused to abase himself by begging his former employee for a job. | humble, demean |

## Day 9: Wealth and poverty, spending and saving

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| affluent | (adj) wealthy | The affluent suburb was full of large houses and expensive cars. | opulent |
| opulent | (adj) rich and luxurious | The opulent palace had gold ceilings and marble floors. | lavish |
| lavish | (adj, v) rich and generous; to give in large amounts | They held a lavish wedding with a thousand guests and a ten-course dinner. | opulent, extravagant |
| prodigal | (adj) wastefully extravagant with money | The prodigal heir spent his entire inheritance in two years. | profligate, spendthrift |
| profligate | (adj) recklessly wasteful; also immoral | The profligate government borrowed billions for projects nobody needed. | prodigal |
| extravagant | (adj) spending or costing far too much | Buying a third sports car seemed extravagant even to his rich friends. | lavish, prodigal |
| spendthrift | (n) a person who wastes money | A spendthrift by nature, he was broke a week after every payday. | prodigal, profligate |
| munificent | (adj) extremely generous | A munificent donor paid for the entire new hospital wing. | magnanimous |
| magnanimous | (adj) generous and forgiving, especially to a rival | Magnanimous in victory, she praised the campaign of her defeated opponent. | munificent |
| squander | (v) to waste foolishly | He squandered his savings on lottery tickets. | waste, fritter |
| frugal | (adj) careful with money; economical | A frugal student, she cooked at home and walked everywhere to save money. | thrifty |
| thrifty | (adj) using money carefully | Thrifty shoppers wait for the sales before buying winter coats. | frugal |
| parsimonious | (adj) extremely unwilling to spend money | The parsimonious owner refused to heat the office even in January. | miserly |
| miserly | (adj) hating to spend money; stingy | The miserly old man kept a fortune in the bank while wearing torn shoes. | parsimonious |
| austere | (adj) severely plain; strict and without comfort | The monk's austere room held only a bed, a chair and a candle. | spartan, stark |

| avarice | (n) extreme greed for wealth | Driven by avarice, the banker risked his clients' savings for a larger bonus. | greed |
| solvent | (adj) able to pay one's debts | The loan kept the company solvent through a year of falling sales. | creditworthy |

## Day 10: Ordinary words with a second meaning the GRE loves

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| qualify | (v) to limit or soften a statement | She qualified her praise by adding that the plan still had serious gaps. | temper, modify |
| table | (v) to postpone discussion of (US usage) | The committee tabled the proposal until next year, so no vote was taken. | shelve, defer |
| flag | (v) to grow tired or weaker | After six hours of climbing, their energy began to flag and the pace slowed. | wane |
| check | (v, n) to stop or restrain; a restraint | The new law was designed to check the growth of monopolies. | stem, curtail |
| arrest | (v) to stop or slow a process; to catch attention | The drug arrested the spread of the infection within days. | check, stem |
| want | (n, v) a lack; to lack | The project failed for want of funds, not for want of effort. | dearth, paucity |
| temper | (v) to make less extreme; to moderate | He tempered his criticism with a few kind words about her effort. | qualify, mitigate |
| sanction | (v, n) to approve officially; also a penalty | The board sanctioned the merger, so the deal could finally go ahead. | authorize, endorse |
| appropriate | (v) to take for one's own use, often without permission | The general appropriated the villagers' grain to feed his army. | seize, commandeer |
| intimate | (v) to hint or suggest indirectly | She intimated that she might resign, though she never said so directly. | insinuate, allude |
| exact | (v) to demand and obtain by force or authority | The gang exacted payment from every shopkeeper on the street. | extort, demand |
| champion | (v) to support or defend a cause | The senator championed the rights of workers throughout her career. | advocate, espouse |
| pedestrian | (adj) dull and ordinary | The film's pedestrian plot offered nothing that viewers had not seen a hundred times. | prosaic, mundane |
| patent | (adj) obvious; plain to see | It was a patent lie, since everyone had watched him take the money. | manifest, blatant |
| discriminating | (adj) having fine judgment or taste | A discriminating reader, she could tell a great novel from a merely good one. | discerning |
| tender | (v) to offer formally | The minister tendered his resignation the morning after the scandal broke. | offer, submit |
| husband | (v) to use carefully and sparingly | Lost in the desert, they husbanded their water and drank only a sip an hour. | conserve |
| court | (v) to try to win favor; to risk inviting something bad | By ignoring the safety rules, the climbers were courting disaster. | invite, woo |
| stem | (v) to stop the flow of; also stem from = to come from | Nurses pressed on the wound to stem the bleeding. | check, arrest |
| weather | (v) to come safely through a difficulty | The small shop weathered the recession and even grew afterwards. | withstand, endure |
