# Words: Week 5 (Days 21 to 25)

Slightly harder words about preference, character, obedience, secrecy and cause.

## Day 21: Liking, disliking and tendencies

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| penchant | (n) a strong liking | He has a penchant for spicy food and adds chili to everything. | predilection |
| predilection | (n) a preference for something | Her predilection for old films meant she rarely watched anything made after 1960. | penchant |
| proclivity | (n) a natural tendency, often toward something bad | His proclivity for gambling cost him two houses. | propensity |
| propensity | (n) a natural tendency to behave in a certain way | Cats have a propensity to sleep in warm places. | proclivity |
| affinity | (n) a natural liking or similarity | She felt an affinity with the sea, having grown up beside it. | rapport, kinship |
| partisan | (adj, n) strongly biased toward one side; a supporter | The partisan crowd booed every decision that went against the home team. | biased |
| enamored | (adj) filled with love or liking for | Enamored of the city, he extended his visit from a week to a year. | captivated |
| relish | (v, n) to enjoy greatly; great enjoyment | She relished the challenge and asked for an even harder problem. | savor |
| antipathy | (n) a strong feeling of dislike | His antipathy toward meetings was so strong that he scheduled none for a year. | aversion, animosity |
| aversion | (n) a strong dislike that makes one avoid something | Her aversion to flying meant she always took the train. | antipathy |
| loath | (adj) unwilling; reluctant (not loathe) | He was loath to admit his mistake and delayed for days before confessing. | reluctant |
| loathe | (v) to hate intensely | She loathes dishonesty and ended the friendship over a single lie. | abhor |
| abhor | (v) to regard with disgust and hatred | A lifelong pacifist, he abhors violence of every kind. | loathe |
| repugnant | (adj) extremely distasteful; offensive | The idea of eating insects was repugnant to him, and he pushed the plate away. | odious |
| odious | (adj) deserving hatred; very unpleasant | Cleaning the drains was an odious task that everyone tried to avoid. | repugnant |
| anathema | (n) something intensely disliked | Raising taxes is anathema to the party, which has opposed every increase. | abomination |
| misanthrope | (n) a person who dislikes people in general | A misanthrope, he lived alone on the hill and chased away every visitor. | hater of mankind |
| indifferent | (adj) having no interest or preference | Indifferent to fashion, she wore the same gray coat for ten years. | apathetic |
| inured | (adj) used to something unpleasant | Nurses become inured to the sight of blood after a few months on the ward. | hardened |
| impervious | (adj) not affected by; not letting anything through | Impervious to criticism, he carried on exactly as before. | immune |

## Day 22: Good and bad character, guilt and forgiveness

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| probity | (n) complete honesty and integrity | A judge of unquestioned probity, she had never accepted even a small gift. | rectitude |
| rectitude | (n) morally correct behavior | His moral rectitude was such that he returned the extra change every time. | probity |
| altruistic | (adj) unselfishly concerned for others | In an altruistic act, the stranger donated a kidney to a child he had never met. | benevolent |
| benevolent | (adj) kind and wishing to do good | The benevolent landlord lowered rents when his tenants lost their jobs. | altruistic |
| benign | (adj) gentle; harmless | The tumor proved benign, so no further treatment was needed. | innocuous |
| nefarious | (adj) wicked or criminal | Police uncovered a nefarious scheme to poison the city's water supply. | wicked |
| depraved | (adj) morally corrupt | The depraved ruler tortured prisoners for his own amusement. | corrupt, wicked |
| venal | (adj) willing to be bribed; corrupt (not venial) | The venal inspector approved the unsafe building in exchange for cash. | corrupt |
| turpitude | (n) wickedness; depravity | He was dismissed for moral turpitude after the fraud came to light. | depravity |
| perfidious | (adj) disloyal; treacherous | The perfidious adviser sold the king's plans to the enemy. | treacherous |
| malevolent | (adj) wishing harm to others | With a malevolent grin, the villain watched the bridge collapse. | malicious |
| pernicious | (adj) harmful in a gradual or hidden way | The pernicious habit of skipping sleep slowly ruined his health. | insidious, deleterious |
| insidious | (adj) spreading harm slowly and unnoticed | The disease is insidious and shows no symptoms until it is far advanced. | pernicious |
| egregious | (adj) outstandingly bad | It was an egregious error, the kind no trained pilot should ever make. | flagrant |
| reprehensible | (adj) deserving strong blame | Stealing from a charity is reprehensible, whatever the thief's excuse. | deplorable |
| culpable | (adj) deserving blame; guilty | The court found the driver culpable because he had ignored a red light. | blameworthy |
| exonerate | (v) to clear from blame | The video exonerated the suspect by showing that he was elsewhere at the time. | exculpate, absolve |
| exculpate | (v) to show or declare free of guilt | The new testimony exculpated the nurse by proving she had followed every rule. | exonerate, vindicate |
| absolve | (v) to free from guilt or responsibility | The report absolved the pilot and blamed the crash on a faulty part. | exonerate |
| contrite | (adj) feeling sincere regret for a wrong | Clearly contrite, he apologized in tears and offered to pay for the damage. | remorseful, penitent |

## Day 23: Flattery and obedience vs. rebellion and disrespect

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| obsequious | (adj) too eager to please or obey | The obsequious assistant laughed at every joke his boss made. | servile, fawning |
| servile | (adj) behaving like a slave; too willing to serve | His servile bowing and scraping embarrassed the other guests. | obsequious, subservient |
| fawning | (adj) seeking favor by excessive flattery | Fawning courtiers told the king that his terrible poem was a work of genius. | obsequious |
| sycophant | (n) a person who flatters the powerful for gain | Surrounded by sycophants, the director never heard an honest opinion. | flatterer, toady |
| ingratiate | (v) to gain favor by deliberate effort | He tried to ingratiate himself with the new manager by bringing her coffee daily. | curry favor |
| cajole | (v) to persuade by flattery or gentle urging | She cajoled her father into lending her the car with compliments and promises. | coax |
| coax | (v) to persuade gently and patiently | He coaxed the frightened cat out from under the bed with a piece of fish. | cajole |
| subservient | (adj) too ready to obey; less important | She refused to play a subservient role and demanded an equal vote. | servile |
| iconoclast | (n) a person who attacks accepted beliefs | An iconoclast, the critic mocked the very writers that everyone else admired. | maverick |
| maverick | (n) an independent person who does not follow the group | A maverick in her party, she voted against its leaders whenever she disagreed. | iconoclast |
| heterodox | (adj) not following accepted beliefs | His heterodox views on medicine got him expelled from the society of doctors. | unorthodox |
| renegade | (n) a person who deserts a group or cause | The renegade officer left the army and joined the rebels. | deserter, traitor |
| eccentric | (adj, n) unconventional and slightly strange | The eccentric professor wore slippers to lectures and kept a parrot in his office. | idiosyncratic |
| aberrant | (adj) departing from the normal or accepted | The aberrant result, ten times higher than the rest, was traced to a faulty sensor. | anomalous |
| insubordinate | (adj) refusing to obey orders | The insubordinate soldier was punished for ignoring a direct command. | recalcitrant |
| insolent | (adj) rudely disrespectful | The insolent pupil rolled his eyes and told the teacher she was boring. | impudent |
| impudent | (adj) cheeky; not showing due respect | It was impudent of the trainee to tell the surgeon how to hold the knife. | insolent |
| irreverent | (adj) lacking respect for things usually taken seriously | The comedian's irreverent jokes about the church shocked older viewers. | flippant |
| flippant | (adj) not serious enough about a serious matter | His flippant reply that nobody cared angered the grieving family. | irreverent |
| flout | (v) to openly disobey a rule (not flaunt) | Drivers who flout the speed limit will lose their licenses. | defy |

## Day 24: Hidden, obvious, hinted and revealed

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| clandestine | (adj) kept secret, often because illegal | The lovers held clandestine meetings in a cafe far from their homes. | covert, surreptitious |
| covert | (adj) secret; not openly shown | The agency ran a covert operation that even the president did not know about. | clandestine |
| surreptitious | (adj) done secretly to avoid notice | He took a surreptitious look at his phone under the table during dinner. | furtive |
| furtive | (adj) secretive in a way that suggests guilt | With a furtive glance over his shoulder, the boy slipped the sweets into his pocket. | surreptitious |
| latent | (adj) present but not yet active or visible | The coach saw latent talent in the shy girl, which emerged a year later. | dormant |
| dormant | (adj) temporarily inactive | The volcano has been dormant for 300 years but could erupt again. | latent |
| tacit | (adj) understood without being stated | Her silence was taken as tacit approval of the plan. | implicit |
| implicit | (adj) suggested but not directly said | There was an implicit threat in his remark that accidents can happen. | tacit |
| overt | (adj) done openly; not hidden | There was no overt hostility, only cold politeness. | open, plain |
| conspicuous | (adj) easy to see; attracting attention | Her bright red coat made her conspicuous in the crowd of gray suits. | salient |
| salient | (adj) most noticeable or important | The salient point of the report is that costs have doubled. | conspicuous |
| blatant | (adj) done openly and without shame | It was a blatant foul, committed right in front of the referee. | flagrant, patent |
| flagrant | (adj) shockingly obvious and offensive | Parking across the hospital entrance was a flagrant breach of the rules. | blatant, egregious |
| divulge | (v) to reveal private or secret information | The journalist refused to divulge the name of her source. | disclose |
| allude | (v) to refer to indirectly | He alluded to past troubles but gave no details. | intimate |
| insinuate | (v) to suggest something unpleasant indirectly | She insinuated that he had cheated, without ever saying it outright. | intimate |
| evince | (v) to show clearly | The pupils evinced no interest in the lesson and stared out of the window. | manifest |
| elicit | (v) to draw out a response (not illicit) | The joke elicited only a few polite smiles. | evoke |
| ostensible | (adj) stated or appearing to be true, but perhaps not | The ostensible reason for his visit was business, but he really came to see her. | purported |
| purported | (adj) claimed, but not proven | The purported cure was never tested in any trial. | ostensible |

## Day 25: Causing, starting and predicting

| Word | Meaning | Example sentence | Close synonyms |
|---|---|---|---|
| engender | (v) to cause or give rise to | The unfair ruling engendered anger across the country. | produce, breed |
| foment | (v) to stir up trouble | Agents were sent to foment unrest among the factory workers. | incite, instigate |
| incite | (v) to encourage violent or unlawful behavior | The speaker was arrested for inciting the crowd to burn the building. | foment |
| instigate | (v) to start or provoke an action | The union instigated the strike after talks failed. | incite |
| goad | (v) to provoke into action | His friends goaded him into jumping by calling him a coward. | spur |
| spur | (v, n) to encourage; an incentive | Tax cuts spurred investment, and new factories opened within months. | galvanize |
| precipitate | (v, adj) to cause to happen suddenly; hasty | The assassination precipitated a war that no one had planned. | trigger |
| catalyst | (n) something that causes or speeds up change | The scandal was the catalyst for reform and forced parliament to act at last. | impetus |
| impetus | (n) a force that makes something happen faster | The prize money gave fresh impetus to research on batteries. | catalyst, spur |
| harbinger | (n) a sign that something is coming | The first swallow is a harbinger of spring. | precursor |
| precursor | (n) something that comes before and leads to another | The telegraph was a precursor of the telephone. | harbinger |
| portend | (v) to be a warning sign of | Dark clouds portend a storm. | presage, augur |
| presage | (v) to be a sign of a future event | The sharp fall in orders presaged a difficult year for the factory. | portend |
| augur | (v) to be a sign of a good or bad outcome | The team's strong start augurs well for the rest of the season. | portend, presage |
| ominous | (adj) suggesting that something bad will happen | An ominous silence fell just before the building shook. | threatening |
| auspicious | (adj) suggesting future success | Sunshine on the opening day was an auspicious start to the festival. | propitious |
| propitious | (adj) favorable; likely to bring success | With prices low, it was a propitious moment to buy a house. | auspicious |
| prescient | (adj) knowing or predicting events before they happen | Her prescient warning about the bank, made two years before it failed, was ignored. | far-sighted |
| culminate | (v) to reach a final or highest point | Months of protest culminated in the president's resignation. | climax |
| advent | (n) the arrival of something important | The advent of the internet changed how people shop, learn and talk. | arrival |
